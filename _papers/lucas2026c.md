When thinking about public opinion, politicians commonly exhibit three general perceptual errors: a tendency to overestimate their constituents’ conservatism (*conservative bias*), a tendency to hold more accurate knowledge of privileged constituents than constituents in general (*privilege bias*), and a tendency to overestimate how much their constituents share their views (*projection bias*). Studies documenting these patterns invariably rely on assessing politicians’ perceptions of aggregated public opinion. In practice, however, politicians heavily prioritize and rely on the opinions of individual constituents, raising concerns about the external validity of these findings. Thus, in this paper, we explore whether politicians’ common perceptual errors persist when they think about specific individual constituents, rather than aggregate public opinion. We assign 1,400 Canadian politicians to predict the ideology and issue positions of more than 4,500 randomly-generated citizen profiles that vary by a host of salient sociodemographic and political factors. We then compare their evaluations to estimates of these citizens’ real-life positions drawn from a general population survey. We find that politicians do not exhibit conservative bias or privilege bias when making inferences about individual constituents. However, even when thinking about individual constituents, projection bias persists, and the perception errors it creates are amplified when politicians are more similar to their constituents. We discuss the implications of these findings for theories of political representation.
