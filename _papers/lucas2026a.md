Recent research in many countries has found that urban elections and policy debates are consistently structured by left–right ideological disagreement. Yet many urban scholars have long argued the opposite: that municipal politics in cities are characterized by largely non-ideological coalitions of residents and local interests. In this Article, we bridge these perspectives by exploring the relationship between ideology and geographic proximity. We hypothesize that ideology structures individual attitudes about municipal policy issues, but that its influence weakens when residents think about issues in the geographically proximate context of their own neighborhoods. We test these hypotheses using a topic sampling experiment that covers 40 municipal policy issues and incorporates more than 40,000 policy preferences from 4,000 distinct survey respondents in Canada. We find clear evidence of ideological disagreement, which is significantly dampened when respondents consider those same issues at the neighborhood scale. Our results shed light on how the ‘localness’ of political settings shapes policy attitudes in urban contexts and advance our understanding of the sources of policy disagreement in urban politics.
